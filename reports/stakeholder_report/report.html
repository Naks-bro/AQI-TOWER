Opening
What if a simple idea about
cleaner air could be explored
like a real engineering system?
The AQI Tower project began with a visual concept: a vertical tower-shaped machine that moves indoor air through multiple treatment stages to deliver cleaner air. Rather than immediately building something and hoping it worked, the team chose a different path.
AI tools helped organize research, inspect technical documents, automate workflows, and accelerate visualization. OpenFOAM simulated airflow in computational detail. Real manufacturer data replaced estimated numbers. Each result — including uncomfortable ones — changed the design.
This report tells that story honestly: what was built, what was discovered, what was changed as a result, and what remains to be done by experienced professionals and physical testing.
"Simulation is most valuable when it changes the design."
— Core principle of this project

The Problem We Are Addressing
Air contains many
different types of
pollutants.
"AQI" — the Air Quality Index — is a number that reflects several different pollutants simultaneously. No single technology removes all of them, and different technologies target different particle sizes and chemical species.
Understanding this distinction drove every major architecture decision in this project.
Fine Particles (PM2.5)
Particles smaller than 2.5 µm. Penetrate deep into lungs. Require HEPA-grade mechanical filtration. Cannot be removed by activated carbon alone.
H13 HEPA target
Gases & VOCs (e.g. HCHO)
Molecules like formaldehyde pass straight through HEPA filters unchanged. Require adsorption media (activated carbon) or chemistry-specific treatment.
Carbon stage research target
Coarse Dust (PM10+)
Larger particles settle quickly. A washable panel pre-filter handles these efficiently, protecting fine filters from rapid clogging.
Phase 18: G4/M5 pre-filter recommended
Why this matters: Every architecture decision — filter type, treatment sequence, carbon media, fan power — follows from this pollutant physics. Getting it wrong produces a device that looks credible but does not address the health-relevant fraction.

A vertical tower.
Multiple treatment stages.
One continuous airflow.
The concept is straightforward: ambient air enters one side, passes through a sequence of treatment stages driven by a fan, and cleaner air exits from the top.
The original treatment sequence proposed in concept documents included particle filtration, activated carbon for gas-phase treatment, an experimental water-based stage, sensors, and eventually solar-assisted power. These were hypotheses, not validated choices.

Airflow — Our First Big Lesson
A good-looking airflow path is not necessarily a good airflow path.
The first airflow simulations (V00, placeholder 1 m/s inlet) revealed that the original tower geometry produced strong recirculation in the riser, uneven velocity distribution, and pressure losses that would reduce the fan's working margin significantly.
Phase V01 introduced the actual Systemair KVO 250 fan performance curve — six data points from the manufacturer catalogue. Instead of assuming a fixed inlet velocity, the simulation now found its own operating point where fan output matched system resistance.
Key finding from V01: At the real fan operating point, the empty tower produced 17.33 Pa of total pressure loss — modest but real. The riser showed 23.1% reverse-flow by volume, indicating significant recirculation that would impair performance.

HEPA Filtration Research
H13 HEPA — what the classification actually means
The project selected the Freudenberg SF13-B-0593×0593×292 as the research candidate HEPA filter. At 593 × 593 × 292 mm, it matches the tower cross-section with a gross face area of 0.351 m².
H13 is a classification under EN 1822. It means the filter achieves ≥99.95% efficiency at the Most Penetrating Particle Size (MPPS) — the one size that is hardest to capture. For glass fibre HEPA, MPPS is typically 0.1–0.25 µm.
What H13 does not automatically mean
An H13 classification on the filter component does not directly translate to 99.95% PM2.5 removal for the complete tower. System performance depends on: bypass leakage paths, face velocity differences from test conditions, frame sealing, and airflow distribution uniformity.
Particle-size efficiency data for this specific product are not publicly available. The EN 1822 test certificate accompanying each purchased unit contains the actual fractional efficiency curve and MPPS — this document must be obtained on purchase.

The Bypass Discovery — Phase 10A/10B
The simulation found something we had not intended to design.
When the first filter model was added to the GEO_C geometry, the simulation revealed that 45% of all simulated airflow was bypassing the filter face — travelling around the filter through an unintended open path at the junction between the dirty plenum and the clean riser.
This was not an assumed simplification. The path was geometrically real in the model. Unfiltered air reached the clean side of the system.
The team investigated the geometry, identified the exact bypass location (z = 0.850 m, x = 0.260–0.305 m overlap zone), designed a corrective baffle — the bypassSeal plate — and reran the simulation.

Particle Tracking Study — Phase 12B
We released simulated particles into a frozen airflow field and followed where they travelled.
Phase 12B injected 224 seed particles per size class at the tower inlet — representing particles of 0.3, 1.0, 2.5, and 10 µm diameter — and tracked their paths through the CASE_M_SEALED velocity field using an equilibrium-slip Euler model.
The model includes gravity but not turbulent dispersion. It is a conservative, simplified model — not a full Lagrangian simulation with stochastic turbulence. Results show where particles tend to travel, not where every real particle will go.

The Most Important Particle Result Caveat
Phase 12B summary — all four particle sizes.
SIMULATION ONLY — NOT PHYSICALLY VALIDATED
FILTER ARRIVAL
≠
FILTER CAPTURE
62% of simulated particles reaching the filter face means exactly that: 62% arrived at the plane where the filter is located. It does not mean 62% were captured, and it says nothing about what percentage of particles were removed from the air.
Filter capture efficiency — the fraction actually retained in the filter media — depends on the product's fractional efficiency curve η(d_p). This data is not yet available for the Freudenberg SF13-B (Phase 12A finding). Without η(d_p), no PM removal efficiency can be calculated.
Similarly, the 37–39% of particles that contacted walls or frame — this is a model output about simulated contact events. Whether real particles would genuinely deposit there, or re-entrain into airflow, depends on particle composition, surface energy, and airflow fluctuations not modelled here.
Why this matters: Oversimplifying particle tracking results into a CADR or efficiency claim would be technically indefensible. The particle study is most useful for understanding geometry, flow topology, and identifying regions of the design that deserve investigation.

Gas-Phase Treatment Research
HEPA filters are invisible to gas molecules. A completely separate treatment mechanism is needed.
Formaldehyde (HCHO), benzene, toluene, and other volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are molecular in size — several orders of magnitude smaller than the particles that HEPA captures. They pass through HEPA media unchanged.
Gas-phase removal requires adsorption — the process by which molecules bind to the surface of a porous solid medium. Activated carbon is the most established material for this purpose.
After evaluating multiple gas species (formaldehyde, benzene, toluene, acetone, ethanol, generic TVOC), Phase 14 selected formaldehyde (HCHO) as the primary research target. It ranked first in a documented weighted decision matrix on health significance, indoor prevalence, measurability, and research tractability.
The primary carbon candidate is Calgon Carbon OVC 4×8 — treated as a non-impregnated research control. A secondary candidate (FORMASORB) is a manufacturer-claimed HCHO-specific impregnated carbon.

Engineering Integrity — Gas CFD Decision
Sometimes the correct engineering decision is not to simulate yet.
To model how a packed carbon bed behaves in CFD, two things are essential: a pressure-drop coefficient describing how the media resists airflow (Darcy–Forchheimer coefficients a and b), and ideally some mass-transfer data describing how quickly HCHO adsorbs.
Neither set of data exists yet for OVC 4×8 at the conditions relevant to this tower. The manufacturer publishes a pressure graph, but it does not cover the velocity ranges this tower uses, and no HCHO mass-transfer coefficient has been published for this specific grade at these conditions.
Rather than inventing coefficients, the project designed a physical experiment to measure them. This is why gas-stage CFD is blocked: blocked for the right reason.
The gas-stage CFD model will be built once real measured inputs are available. Running it with invented numbers would produce a credible-looking but meaningless result.

Water-Spray Stage — Phase 18 Decision
The original concept included a water-spray stage. Phase 18 reviewed it rigorously. The result was NO-GO.
Phase 18 was a research-only engineering feasibility assessment — no CFD, no physical experiment. The analysis reviewed aerosol physics, pressure-drop impact, biological risk standards, water handling complexity, and alternatives.
The core physics problem: simple low-energy spray towers are ineffective for PM2.5 (0.1–2 µm). This is the "Greenfield gap" — the particle size range where neither inertial impaction nor diffusion produces useful collection in a compact chamber.
Why the water stage was rejected
PM2.5 irrelevant: Greenfield gap — simple spray cannot capture 0.3–2.5 µm particles at this scale
Coarse dust better served by dry G4/M5 pre-filter — same function, no water, no risk
Hygiene risk is disqualifying: Legionella and biofilm in recirculating warm-water spray (ASHRAE 188, CIBSE TM13)
Carryover threatens HEPA: droplets reaching filter face cause wetting and microbial loading
35–125 Pa added ΔP — reduces airflow 7–23% from an already-constrained operating point
12+ extra components — pump, tank, nozzles, demister — for no unique benefit
Replacement: G4/M5 washable pre-filter
Achieves coarse pre-collection upstream of HEPA. No water, no pump, no Legionella risk. One component. ~25–50 Pa clean ΔP. Washable monthly. Widely available in India at low cost.
New treatment sequence: Inlet → [G4/M5 washable pre-filter] → [Activated carbon bed] → [H13 HEPA] → Clean riser → KVO 250 fan → Outlet

Eight Lessons from the Engineering Process
01
A good-looking airflow path is not necessarily a good airflow path.
V00 had plausible visual flow but significant recirculation and pressure losses. Three geometry iterations were needed to reach a well-behaved field.
02
A fan must be evaluated with system resistance.
Adding real fan curve data (V01) fundamentally changed the simulation. A fan delivers the flow at which system resistance equals fan output — not a fixed number.
03
Filters create large pressure penalties that shift the operating point.
The H13 HEPA added ~111 Pa — reducing airflow from ~1,496 to ~1,332 m³/h. Every treatment stage competes for the same fan headroom.
04
Bypass paths matter as much as filter efficiency.
45% bypass — discovered in simulation — showed that a highly rated filter can be functionally irrelevant if air routes around it. Finding this early was the value of the simulation.
05
Particle trajectories are not filtration efficiency.
62% of simulated particles reaching the filter face is a geometry-routing result, not a removal efficiency. No capture probability was applied because it is not yet available.
06
Gas removal is pollutant-specific and requires pollutant-specific evidence.
A documented comparison of six gas species was needed before HCHO could be selected. Generic "carbon removes VOCs" is not an engineering claim.
07
Missing data should lead to an experiment — not an invented coefficient.
Gas-phase CFD was blocked, correctly. The packed-bed experiment exists specifically to generate the inputs that will make CFD meaningful.
08
A stage that looks useful can be eliminated by physics.
The water-spray stage was part of the original concept. Phase 18 showed that basic aerosol physics makes it ineffective for PM2.5 while introducing serious hygiene risks. Removing it was correct.

The Value of the Work So Far
The result is not a finished purifier.
The result is a better-defined engineering problem.
Before this work, the AQI Tower was a visual concept — a plausible shape with no engineering basis for any of its parameters, filter choices, fan selection, treatment stages, or performance claims.
After this work, the team knows: why a particular geometry produces less pressure loss; where bypass was hiding; what the fan curve looks like; what particle trajectory data can and cannot tell you; which gas is the right research target; and what experiment is needed before gas-stage CFD can be trusted.
None of these answers existed before the work began. All of them reduce the cost and risk of physical development.

Claims We Do Not Make
Responsible engineering means being equally clear about what you do not know.
No validated CADR
Clean Air Delivery Rate requires physical measurement under standard conditions. No CADR value has been established for the AQI Tower at any stage of development.
No PM2.5 removal efficiency
The H13 classification is a component rating at test conditions. Without η(d_p) at operating velocity, no system-level PM2.5 efficiency can be calculated from available data.
No HCHO removal efficiency
No breakthrough experiment has been performed. The carbon stage has no measured capacity, no breakthrough time, and no gas-phase CADR.
No claim of physical bypass = 0%
CFD bypass ≈ 0% is a modelled result. Whether a fabricated tower is physically airtight depends entirely on manufacturing quality — not CFD.
62% is not particle removal
62% is a simulated particle arrival fraction at the filter plane. Filter capture efficiency η(d_p) has not been applied — it is not available for this product.
1,332 m³/h is not measured airflow
1,332 m³/h is a RANS CFD result from a partially converged case. It is a useful screening estimate. Real tower airflow has never been measured.
